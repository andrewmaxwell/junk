What is this?

Lambda calculus is a programming language with only one thing in it: functions. No numbers, no true or false, no loops. Alonzo Church invented it in 1936 to answer a question at the foundations of math: is there a mechanical procedure that can decide, for any statement, whether it can be proved? (There isn’t. Alan Turing showed the same thing with his machines that year.) It turns out that anything a computer can compute, lambda calculus can compute too. It’s the core of functional programming languages like Haskell, and proof assistants like Lean are built on typed versions of it. The lessons build true and false, numbers, arithmetic, and recursion out of nothing but functions.

Bret Victor’s Alligator Eggs turns it into a game:

A hungry alligator eats whatever is just to its right.

Each egg belongs to the alligator of its color above it.

After eating, the alligator dies, and each of its eggs hatches into a copy of what it ate.

Old alligators hold a group together. When only one thing is left in it, the old alligator leaves.

In the usual notation, written under the picture, an alligator is a function, written λx. followed by its family. An egg is a variable like x, and its color says which λ it belongs to. What’s to an alligator’s right is what the function is applied to, and eating is applying it: replacing the variable with the argument everywhere it appears (called β-reduction). Old alligators are parentheses, and names like TRUE or 2 are shorthand that opens up when it’s needed.

Press Next (or space) to go one step at a time, or ⏩ Fast forward to play the rest quickly. Between steps, you can click any hungry alligator with something to its right to choose who eats next.
