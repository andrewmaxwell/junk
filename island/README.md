Island - 2026 - Millions of years in seconds: an island rises, rivers carve its valleys, climate paints it, and as it sinks, coral turns it into an atoll.

The crust under the island rises, rivers cut down into it, and soil creeps downhill, following the standard landscape evolution equation

dh/dt = U − K Q^m · S + D ∇²h

where U is uplift, Q is the river's discharge (all the rain falling upstream of a point), S is the slope to the next point downstream and D is how fast soil creeps. River cutting is solved implicitly down the drainage tree (Braun & Willett 2013, the method behind FastScape), so it is stable with 2,000-year steps. Hollows fill into lakes that spill over their lowest rim (priority flood, Barnes 2014), and rivers end at the sea, which they can't cut below.

What the rivers cut away travels downstream and settles in still water: lakes fill in from where rivers enter, and at the coast it spreads down a gentle slope from each river mouth into a shelf. Rain comes in on the wind. Air climbing the island's broad slopes condenses into cloud, which rains out as it drifts on over about a kilometer, so rain spills a little way past each ridge and fades into the lee instead of stopping at the crest. The air dries out as it crosses, so the windward side is wet and the lee is dry. It cools 6.5 °C per kilometer of height. Plants and soil send part of the rain back to the air, following Budyko's curve, so only the rest reaches rivers: in hot, dry places that's almost none on average, though bare ground still sheds up to 30% of its rain in storms. Plant cover follows how the rain compares with what warm air could take up, and roots hold soil, so bare ground erodes about 7 times faster than forest. Biomes come from the same two numbers (Whittaker's biomes, with UNEP's aridity bands): rainforest, forest, conifer forest, savanna, grassland, dry scrub, desert, tundra, and ice and snow. The **Rain** setting is the average over the land; tap a spot to see what grows there and how much rain falls. Turn down the rain to watch the lee become desert badlands, or the temperature to bring tundra down to the coast and snow onto the peaks.

`node island/test.js` checks that at steady state every river's slope matches the exact answer U / (K Q^m), that sediment is conserved to a part in 10¹³, that the windward side is wetter, that Budyko's evaporation hits its known points, that erosion peaks in semi-arid climates, as Langbein and Schumm found in real river basins (near 300 mm of rain a year), because there's runoff to move soil but too little for plants to hold it, that an island sinking 0.5 mm a year becomes an atoll (and sinks 170 m deeper without reefs), and that sinking faster than reefs grow drowns them.

Tap or click the land to follow a raindrop to the sea and see how much water flows past. Pick **Raise** or **Lower** to sculpt (drag to paint; on a phone, two fingers still zoom and pan), and watch the rivers find new routes. Lower the sea to expose the shelf, or raise it to drown the valleys. **World** picks a preset: a rising island, a sinking one that becomes an atoll, an ice age, a desert or an arctic island. Each changes the climate and keeps the island, so you watch it adjust. Tap the sea to see whether coral grows there and why not. The camera drifts around the island until you take it. Space pauses. The URL keeps the island's seed and any changed settings.

The simulation runs on a 256 or 384 point grid (33–50 m apart). The GPU draws the ground three times finer, interpolates the simulated heights smoothly, and adds fine ridged relief that's strongest on steep slopes and smoothed out along valley floors. It shades each pixel from the same climate as the simulation: forest canopy, grass with scattered trees, scrub, red desert soils, layered rock, tundra, snow, beaches and reef flats, darkened in hollows by ambient occlusion computed from the heightmap. That detail is decoration, not simulation. Rivers are cut into the ground from a texture four times finer than the grid, along smoothed, slightly meandering paths: a channel at their real width (5 m × √(flow in m³/s), per Leopold and Maddock), deeper for bigger rivers, with sloping banks, gravel bars on the larger ones, a flat valley floor and a strip of thicker growth along the banks, which is what makes them readable from afar. The sea and lakes clear up over shallow ground, and foam breaks on the shore.

In a warm sea (above about 18 °C) coral reefs build up toward the surface, up to 8 mm a year where the water is shallow and clear, falling off with depth as the light does (Bosscher and Schlager's light curve, so little grows below about 50 m), away from river mud (sediment settling faster than about 5 mm a year smothers them) and away from the open ocean, which flushes the reef's edge with fresh water. Set **Uplift** below zero, or pick **Sinking: atoll**, and you can watch Darwin's 1842 sequence: the island sinks (the whole sea floor subsides as the plate cools) and erodes, its fringing reef keeps growing up and becomes a barrier reef around a lagoon, and when the last peak goes under, a ring of reef is left: an atoll. Storms throw rubble from the reef's edge onto the flat behind it and build low islets (motus) up to 3 m high, mostly on the windward side, with gaps where the rubble happens not to pile up. Sink faster than reefs can grow and they drown, leaving a flat-topped seamount. **Ice age** drops the sea 120 m and exposes the reef flats and shelf as a coastal plain.

Lakes are rare and short-lived: on a rising, rainy island, a hollow that forms is usually filled with the first river's sediment within a few thousand years (in one run, 96% of lake cells ended that way, half within one 2,000-year step). Dam a valley with **Raise** to make one that lasts.

Simplifications: sediment only settles in lakes and the sea, so rivers never build floodplains or fans on land; reefs grow from the sea-level air temperature rather than a real sea temperature, the lagoon floor gets no reef sand, and rising ground follows the uplift map while sinking is even everywhere; the rock is the same everywhere; there are no waves eating at the coast; and plants are always in balance with the climate, since they settle in far less than one 2,000-year step. The rain model is a simple sweep along the wind, not a weather simulation, and evaporation is a rough fit to temperature.
