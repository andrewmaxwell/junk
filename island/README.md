Island - 2026 - Millions of years in seconds: an island rises, rivers carve its valleys, climate paints it, and as it sinks, coral turns it into an atoll.

The island is a chain of two or three basalt shield volcanoes, like the islands a hotspot builds as the plate slides over it: the oldest already worn, the youngest still growing, stretched along its rift zones and topped by a caldera. The land rises (mostly lava building up the young volcano), rivers cut down into it and drop part of what they carry, and soil creeps downhill, following the landscape evolution equation

dh/dt = U − K Q^m · S + G Qs / A + D ∇²h

where U is uplift, Q is the river's discharge (all the rain falling upstream of a point), S is the slope to the next point downstream, Qs is the sediment the river carries, A the area draining through it, and D is how fast soil creeps. Cutting and deposition are solved implicitly down the drainage tree (Braun & Willett 2013, the method behind FastScape, with Yuan et al.'s 2019 deposition term), so they are stable with 2,000-year steps. Rivers drop sediment where they slow, so valleys fill with gravel and coastal plains build up where they leave the mountains.

The rock isn't the same everywhere. Young lava is so cracked that rain soaks straight in, so a fresh volcano's flanks stay smooth for a few hundred thousand years while the old one's are carved into deep valleys. Lava flows alternate with soft beds of ash and rubble, so rivers cutting down meet hard and soft layers in turn, and dikes, the hard sheets of basalt that fed the rift zones' eruptions, hold out as ridges. Loose sediment erodes twice as easily as basalt. Ground can only stand so steep, about 48° for rock and 33° for deep sediment: anything steeper slides down as a landslide and leaves loose debris below, so ridges come to sharp crests and valley walls to straight, steep sides, and sea cliffs collapse as waves undercut them. Waves cut the coast back into sea cliffs over a platform a couple of meters under the sea. They build up crossing open water downwind and lose their strength crossing shallows, so the windward coast is cut hardest, and a reef or a wide shelf shelters the coast behind it. Hollows fill into lakes that spill over their lowest rim (priority flood, Barnes 2014), and rivers end at the sea, which they can't cut below.

What the rivers carry past the land settles in still water: lakes fill in from where rivers enter, and at the coast it spreads down a gentle slope from each river mouth into a shelf. Rain comes in on the wind. Air climbing the island's broad slopes condenses into cloud, which rains out as it drifts on over about a kilometer, so rain spills a little way past each ridge and fades into the lee instead of stopping at the crest. The air dries out as it crosses, so the windward side is wet and the lee is dry. It cools 6.5 °C per kilometer of height. Plants and soil send part of the rain back to the air, following Budyko's curve, so only the rest reaches rivers: in hot, dry places that's almost none on average, though bare ground still sheds up to 30% of its rain in storms. Plant cover follows how the rain compares with what warm air could take up, and roots hold soil, so bare ground erodes about 7 times faster than forest. Biomes come from the same two numbers (Whittaker's biomes, with UNEP's aridity bands): rainforest, forest, conifer forest, savanna, grassland, dry scrub, desert, tundra, and ice and snow. The **Rain** setting is the average over the land; tap a spot to see what grows there and how much rain falls. Turn down the rain to watch the lee become desert badlands, or the temperature to bring tundra down to the coast and snow onto the peaks.

`node island/test.js` checks that sediment is conserved to a part in 10¹³, that the windward side is wetter, that Budyko's evaporation hits its known points, that erosion peaks in semi-arid climates, as Langbein and Schumm found in real river basins (near 300 mm of rain a year), because there's runoff to move soil but too little for plants to hold it, that rivers at steady state have exactly the slope Yuan et al.'s solution predicts with deposition, (U + G Ū) / (K Q^m) with Ū the mean uplift upstream, that waves cut the windward coast back further than the lee, that landslides leave almost nothing steeper than rock can stand, that an island sinking 0.5 mm a year becomes an atoll (without reefs, waves plane it off and it drowns 77 m deep), and that sinking faster than reefs grow drowns them.

Tap or click the land to follow a raindrop to the sea and see how much water flows past. Pick **Raise** or **Lower** to sculpt (drag to paint; on a phone, two fingers still zoom and pan), and watch the rivers find new routes. Lower the sea to expose the shelf, or raise it to drown the valleys. **World** picks a preset: a rising island, a sinking one that becomes an atoll, ice-age cycles, a single ice age, a desert or an arctic island. **Ice-age cycles** swing the sea 120 m down and back every 100,000 years, as it has for the last million: slowly down as ice sheets grow, wobbling with the 23,000-year precession cycle, then up in about 12,000 years as they melt. At low stands rivers cut down across the exposed shelf; when the sea comes back it drowns their valleys, and on a rising island each high stand leaves its own wave-cut terrace. Each changes the climate and keeps the island, so you watch it adjust. Tap the sea to see whether coral grows there and why not. The camera drifts around the island until you take it. Space pauses. The URL keeps the island's seed and any changed settings.

The simulation runs on a 256 or 384 point grid (33–50 m apart). The GPU draws the ground three times finer, interpolates the simulated heights smoothly, and adds fine ridged relief that's strongest on steep slopes and smoothed out along valley floors. It shades each pixel from the same climate as the simulation: forest canopy, grass with scattered trees, scrub, red desert soils, tundra, snow, beaches and reef flats, darkened in hollows by ambient occlusion computed from the heightmap. That detail is decoration, not simulation, but the bare rock is the model's: dark lava flows, with the red-brown ash beds exactly where the simulation has them (the finer flow lines within them are decoration), black dikes standing out on ridges, and gravel where landslide debris lies deep. Rivers are cut into the ground from a texture four times finer than the grid, along smoothed, slightly meandering paths: a channel at their real width (5 m × √(flow in m³/s), per Leopold and Maddock), deeper for bigger rivers, with sloping banks, gravel bars on the larger ones, a flat valley floor and a strip of thicker growth along the banks, which is what makes them readable from afar. Sunlight fades through the sea as it does in clear tropical water, red within a few meters and blue over tens, so sand under a meter of water is pale aqua, under five it's turquoise, and past thirty only deep blue is left; in the shallows, waves focus it into a rippling net on the floor. A swell rolls in on the wind, calmer in the island's lee, and breaks white on reef edges and steep shores.

Clouds form where the model condenses them. The same rising air that makes the rain piles cumulus over the windward slopes and peaks, starting about 600 m up, and they stay put while their billows drift with the wind; over the open sea, scattered trade-wind cumulus pass by. They're raymarched through the sky and cast shadows on the land and sea. A desert world has clear skies, and a tall, wet island wears a cap of cloud. **Time of day** moves the sun from dawn to dusk: the light reddens through more air as the sun drops (Kasten and Young's air mass), and the clouds and haze turn pink with it. Untick **Clouds** to see the whole island.

In a warm sea (above about 18 °C) coral reefs build up toward the surface, up to 8 mm a year where the water is shallow and clear, falling off with depth as the light does (Bosscher and Schlager's light curve, so little grows below about 50 m), away from river mud (sediment settling faster than about 5 mm a year smothers them) and away from the open ocean, which flushes the reef's edge with fresh water. Set **Uplift** below zero, or pick **Sinking: atoll**, and you can watch Darwin's 1842 sequence: the island sinks (the whole sea floor subsides as the plate cools) and erodes, its fringing reef keeps growing up and becomes a barrier reef around a lagoon, and when the last peak goes under, a ring of reef is left: an atoll. Storms throw rubble from the reef's edge onto the flat behind it and build low islets (motus) up to 3 m high, mostly on the windward side, with gaps where the rubble happens not to pile up. Sink faster than reefs can grow and they drown, leaving a flat-topped seamount. **Ice age** drops the sea 120 m and exposes the reef flats and shelf as a coastal plain.

Lakes are rare and short-lived: on a rising, rainy island, a hollow that forms is usually filled with the first river's sediment within a few thousand years (in one run, 96% of lake cells ended that way, half within one 2,000-year step). Dam a valley with **Raise** to make one that lasts.

Simplifications: the volcanoes don't erupt, they only rise, and the lava layers and dikes are drawn from noise rather than built flow by flow; rubble from sea cliffs settles at their foot and the rest is lost to the deep sea; reefs grow from the sea-level air temperature rather than a real sea temperature, the lagoon floor gets no reef sand, and rising ground follows the uplift map while sinking is even everywhere; the swell and surf you see are drawn, not the waves the model cuts the coast with; clouds are drawn from the model's cloud water, but they're a picture of it, not a weather simulation; and plants are always in balance with the climate, since they settle in far less than one 2,000-year step. The rain model is a simple sweep along the wind, not a weather simulation, and evaporation is a rough fit to temperature.
