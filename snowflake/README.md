Snowflake - 2026 - Draw the weather a snow crystal falls through and watch it grow in 3D: plates, columns, ferny stars, and everything in between.

Growth follows Gravner and Griffeath's mesoscopic model of snow crystals ("Modeling snow-crystal growth: a three-dimensional mesoscopic approach", 2009) on a lattice of hexagonal prisms, run on WebGPU. Vapor diffuses toward the crystal, condenses on its surface, and cells join the crystal once they've gathered enough, more easily the more crystal they touch. Branching, faceting, ridges and hollows all come out of that. The six-fold symmetry comes from the lattice, as it comes from ice's molecules in real snow, and only 1/24 of the crystal is simulated.

The chart in the corner is temperature across and supersaturation over ice up: how much more water vapor the air holds than ice is in balance with. The dashed line is water saturation. Inside a cloud, droplets keep the air near that line, which is why ferny stars, which need air near water saturation at about -15°C, are common, while the far top of the chart is only reached in a lab. Drag on the chart to draw a path, or click to hold one spot; the dot moves along the path as the crystal grows. Random weather draws a smooth wandering path, so every crystal comes out different. Temperature sets how much harder a new layer is to start on the top face than on the sides (real crystals flip between plates and columns twice between 0 and -22°C), and how close the air is to water saturation sets how much vapor the model has. The region labels follow Libbrecht's morphology diagram, but the mapping from the chart to the model's parameters is hand tuned, so the model's crystals only roughly match where real ones grow.

Each cell remembers when it joined, so Rewind shows the crystal at any earlier moment, with the dot back where the weather was then, and Replay the growth plays it back. The URL keeps the path and grid size, so a link grows the same crystal.

The preview is path traced: rays march through the cells, and at each surface of the ice they either reflect or bend through it, picked at random by Fresnel's odds, until they get out to the light. Averaging the frames adds up every way light gets through the crystal, including light bouncing between branches. Lighting copies how real snowflakes are photographed: dark field (a ring of light behind the crystal, so flat ice looks dark and edges and ridges glow), a colored Rheinberg backlight like Libbrecht's photos, or outdoors on a dark mitten under an overcast sky. Drag to orbit, scroll or pinch to zoom.
